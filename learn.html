  • IsiBheqe soHlamvu is a writing system for the siNtu languages of Southern Africa.
  • It is also called Ditema tsa Dinoko, Xifungho xa Manungu and Vhuga ha Madungo.
  • It is a syllabary: one symbol for each syllable.
  • It is featural: the shape of a symbol shows how your mouth makes the sound.

Sivelaphi isiBheqe?

Where isiBheqe comes from: background and context

Izimpawu zesintu

Symbols: isibheqe.org.za (by Taurai Mtake), CC BY-NC-SA

Show the background

Lolu hlelo lokuloba luvela ebuciko bomdabuko, sithi: ditema tsa Sesotho, igwalo zesiNdebele, namabheqe esiZulu, nezinye izinhlelo zezimpawu zesintu. IsiBheqe SoHlamvu (Ditema tsa Dinoko) siyaqhubeka ngalokhu ngokufanekisela izimpawu ezilimini zethu.

This writing system originates in traditional arts, such as Sesotho mural art, isiNdebele house painting and isiZulu beaded messages, as well as other traditional symbologies. IsiBheqe SoHlamvu (Ditema tsa Dinoko) continues in this by relating the symbols to our languages directly.

IsiBheqe siklanywe ngokusetshenziswa ngezilimi zesintu zonke ezikhulunywa e-Afrika laseningizimu, noma yisiNguni, Sesotho Setswana, Xitsonga, Tshivenḓa, nezinye izigaba zezilimi.

IsiBheqe is designed to be used with all Ntu languages that are spoken in Southern Africa, whether it be Nguni languages, Sotho-Tswana languages, Xitsonga, Tshivenḓa, or other language groups.

  • It helps most where a language has no official spelling: sePulana, isiBhaca, isiHlubi, sePhuthi, isiLala, isiNhlangwini, Northern Transvaal isiNdebele.

IsiBheqe / Ditema: how does it work?

1Ibheqesyllable
Every symbol is a whole syllable
pʰa
Read more
  • English writes one letter per sound: g-e-t is 3 letters.
  • IsiBheqe writes one symbol per syllable.
  • One symbol is an ibheqe. More than one: amabheqe.
  • The symbol here is pha: "give" in isiZulu and Sesotho.
  • Two symbols already make words: i·pha (give), m·pha (give me).
Every ibheqe has two parts: the triangle is the vowel; the marks around it are the consonant.
2Onkamisavowels
The triangle is the vowel
  • The triangle is your mouth. It points where your tongue goes.
  • i and u: the tongue rises, so the triangle points up.
  • a: the tongue drops, so it points down.
  • e and ɛ are made at the front, so they point left (the front of a face looking left).
  • o and ɔ are made at the back, so they point right.

Tap to hear.

Try it: say lilimi, then lala. Feel your tongue rise, then drop.
For e and o, try ewe and oko oko.

3Ongwaqaconsonants
The marks show the consonant

Think of the triangle as your mouth.

The shape of the symbol is made to mimic the shape of the mouth.

  • Every speech sound starts as air from your lungs. You stop it somewhere and somehow: in your throat, your mouth or your nose.
  • The somewhere is the place. The somehow is the manner. Every consonant has both.

Indawo place: where in your mouth

Sounds made at the front of your mouth are written at the tip of the triangle, sounds in the middle in the middle, and sounds at the back at the wide end.

Change the vowel and the marks stay put:

phethekhe

The side of your mouth is a place too:

Indlela manner: how the air moves

Stops (plosives): you stop the air, then let it go. Pha, tha and kha are all stops.

Fricatives: you bring parts of your mouth close, so the air rubs through. Fricative comes from friction.

Affricates: a stop and a fricative together.

pfaPfarelo, forgiveness

Nasals: the air goes out through your nose.

Clicks, taps and the other manners are in the chart.

Articulatory modes: a closer look Optional

The walkthrough showed you where and how. This section zooms in on the extra marks that tell you which version of a sound you're looking at.

Voicing: the line down the middle

Your vocal cords can buzz (voiced) or stay quiet (voiceless) for any consonant. In the symbol, voicing is the uphimbo: a vertical line from the tip of the triangle to its base, like the central post of a house. You can feel your vocal cords vibrate from the first moment of the sound.

Try it: Put your hand on your throat. Say "pha": no vibration at the start. Now say "bha": the vibration starts immediately.

Ejectives: the lerothodi dot (also ilithonsi, iliconsi)

English doesn't have ejectives, but they're common in isiZulu, Sesotho, and Setswana. The air does not come from your lungs. Your throat closes, the air trapped in your mouth is squeezed, and it pops out sharply. Lerothodi, ilithonsi and iliconsi all mean a droplet.

Try it: Say "pha" normally. Now hold your breath tight, close your lips, and release sharply. That popping sensation is close to an ejective.

For isiNtu speakers: You already make ejectives naturally: the p in papadi (Sesotho, "a game") and the k in koko are ejective, while ph and kh are not. The lerothodi marks that version of the sound.
Implosives

An implosive looks voiced, and it is, but there's a subtle extra quality. As the lips close and release, air briefly draws inward before the burst outward. It gives the sound a slightly hollow, sunken quality.

Listen for it: In isiZulu, bha is the plain voiced sound and ba is the implosive. In isiBheqe the implosive carries both marks: the uphimbo (voiced) and the lerothodi (no air from the lungs).

Prenasalisation: the ingungwana

The ingungwana is a circle at the tip of the triangle, touching it. It opens a second doorway for the air: the nose. The air starts out through your nose, then the consonant follows through your mouth, so fast that it is one sound, not two.

Try it: Say inkosi slowly. Feel the n flowing straight into the k? That is prenasalisation: mba, nda, nga in isiNtu are single onset sounds, not clusters.

It also does grammar. In many siNtu languages a nasal at the front turns a verb into a noun. Sesotho ruta (teach) becomes thuto (a lesson): it was once n + ruto, and the nasal changed the r to th before it dropped away.
