The IsiBheqe soHlamvu Syllabary

A simple explainer page with interactive chart

IsiBheqe soHlamvu is a modern, phonetically-based indigenous writing system developed for all Southern African isiNtu languages. It is also known as Ditema tsa Dinoko, Xifhungo xa Manungu, or Vhuga ha Madungo.

▸ Background & context

IsiBheqe/Ditema draws from Southern African traditional art, like Sesotho litema art (used on murals) and isiZulu ucu art (used on beadwork). This art was decorative but also used systems of shapes to convey messages—births, milestones, news for neighbors and travelers.

The script can be used for all Southern African isiNtu languages and dialects. It's especially useful for languages without formal Latin orthography, like sePulana, isiBhaca, isiHlubi, sePhuthi, isiLala, isiNhlangwini, and Northern Transvaal isiNdebele.

In English you write one letter per sound: g-e-t = 3 letters for 3 sounds. IsiBheqe works differently — one symbol captures a full syllable: vowel and consonant bundled together.

A single symbol is called an ibheqe (plural: amabheqe). The symbol to the left is the ibheqe for pʰa — the syllable in pha, meaning "give" in isiZulu and Sesotho. Two symbols already make words: i·pha (give) and m·pha (give me).

Every ibheqe has two parts: a triangle in the middle (the vowel), and marks around it (the consonant). The next two steps explain each.
The triangle is the vowel
Its direction shows where your tongue goes

At the centre of every ibheqe is a triangle — the nucleus. Think of the triangle as your mouth. The direction it points shows where your tongue goes when you make the vowel. For i the tongue rises to the roof of your mouth, so the triangle points up. For a the tongue drops to the floor of your mouth, so it points down. Your jaw only moves to make room: it drops for a because the tongue needs the space.

Try it: say lilimi and feel where your tongue sits. Now say lala. With lilimi your tongue rises to the roof of your mouth; with lala it drops to the floor. That is why i points up and a points down. For e and o, try ewe and oko oko.

The marks show the consonant
Where in your mouth, and how the air moves
Think of the triangle as a house, and the house as your mouth. The tip is your lips: the doorway the breath leaves through, like the smoke-hole at the top of a thatched house. The base is the back of your throat. The sides are the sides of your tongue. A consonant mark shows which part of the house the sound is made in, and what happens there.
① WHERE — Place of articulation

Start with where. The position of the mark around the triangle shows where in the mouth-house the sound is made:

Lips (ndebembili) → pha, ba, ma Teeth/Ridge → ta, da, na Roof of mouth → ja, nya Back of throat → ka, ga
② HOW — Manner of articulation

Then how. The shape of the mark shows how the air moves. There are two basic kinds of sound: a once-off sound, where the air is stopped and released in one pop, and a continuous sound, where the air keeps flowing. Everything else builds on these two:

Small marks change the version
Same place, same shape — a different sound

Your lips make all four of these words the same way. What changes is what your throat does. Two extra marks show it: the uphimbo, a line down the middle of the triangle, means voiced (your throat buzzes); the lerothodi, a small dot, means the air does not come from your lungs. Articulatory modes — a closer look Optional

The walkthrough showed you where and how — this section zooms in on the extra marks that tell you which version of a sound you're looking at. Same place, same shape, different release.

① Voicing — the line down the middle

Your vocal cords can buzz (voiced) or stay quiet (voiceless) for any consonant. In the symbol, voicing is the uphimbo: a vertical line from the tip of the triangle to its base, like the central post of a house. You can feel it as a buzz in your throat from the first moment of the sound.

Try it: Put your hand on your throat. Say "pha" — no buzz at the start. Now say "bha" — the buzz starts immediately.

② Ejectives — the lerothodi dot (also ilithonsi, iliconsi)

English doesn't have ejectives, but they're common in isiZulu, Sesotho, and Setswana. The air does not come from your lungs. Your throat closes, the air trapped in your mouth is squeezed, and it pops out sharply. Lerothodi, ilithonsi and iliconsi all mean a droplet.

Try it: Say "pha" normally. Now hold your breath tight, close your lips, and release sharply — that popping sensation is close to an ejective.

For isiNtu speakers: You already make ejectives naturally: the p in papadi (Sesotho, "a game") and the k in koko are ejective, while ph and kh are not. The lerothodi marks that version of the sound.
③ Implosives

An implosive looks voiced — and it is — but there's a subtle extra quality. As the lips close and release, air briefly draws inward before the burst outward. It gives the sound a slightly hollow, sunken quality.

Listen for it: In isiZulu, bha is the plain voiced sound and ba is the implosive. In isiBheqe the implosive carries both marks: the uphimbo (voiced) and the lerothodi (no air from the lungs).

④ Prenasalisation — the ingungwana

The ingungwana is a circle at the tip of the triangle, touching it. It opens a second doorway for the air: the nose. The air starts out through your nose, then the consonant follows through your mouth, so fast that it is one sound, not two.

Try it: Say inkosi slowly — feel the n flowing straight into the k? That is prenasalisation: mba, nda, nga in isiNtu are single onset sounds, not clusters.

It also does grammar. In many siNtu languages a nasal at the front turns a verb into a noun. Sesotho ruta (teach) becomes thuto (a lesson): it was once n + ruto, and the nasal changed the r to th before it dropped away.

In the chart: Prenasalised consonants appear in their own rows. Look for the ingungwana circle at the apex in the symbol images.
